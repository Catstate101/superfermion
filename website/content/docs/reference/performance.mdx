Superfermion's Rust simulation core delivers significant speedups over popular
quantum computing frameworks in specific regimes. Methodology and per-trial
data live in [`notebooks/benchmarks_walkthrough.ipynb`](https://github.com/Catstate101/superfermion/blob/main/notebooks/benchmarks_walkthrough.ipynb)
(identical circuits, 15 trials, medians reported, GC disabled, correctness
gates on fidelity/distributions).

## Simulation Benchmarks

### Stabilizer Simulation

Clifford-only circuits on the word-packed stabilizer backend.

| Circuit | Qubits | Superfermion (stab) | Qiskit Aer (stab) | Speedup |
|---------|--------|---------------------|--------------------|---------|
| Clifford 500 gates | 100 | 3 ms | 15 ms | **5.0x** |
| Clifford 1000 gates | 100 | 6 ms | 28 ms | **4.7x** |
| QEC surface code | 49 | 2 ms | 12 ms | **6.0x** |

### MPS Tensor Network

Weakly entangled circuits (bond dimension 64).

| Circuit | Qubits | Superfermion (MPS) | Qiskit Aer (MPS) | Speedup |
|---------|--------|-------------------|-------------------|---------|
| Heisenberg XXZ | 50 | 45 ms | 960 ms | **21x** |
| Heisenberg XXZ | 100 | 180 ms | 5,900 ms | **33x** |
| GHZ | 200 | 25 ms | — (OOM) | — |

Note: GHZ is the trivially-best case for MPS (bond dimension 2) — it
demonstrates framework overhead, not tensor-network scaling on heavily
entangled workloads.

### Statevector vs Qulacs (v0.1.15)

56 statevector cases, the `superfermion_optimized` build against Qulacs 0.6.14
on the same host, 4 threads, rotated paired ordering, 2 warmups, medians over
41/21/13/9 repetitions as the case gets slower. All 56 cases agree on the final
state to a maximum absolute difference of `9.4e-16`. The table reports the
regime, not a single cherry-picked point:

| Regime | Measured result |
|---|---|
| n = 4–10 | Qulacs faster at every point, from 7.2x (n=6, 64 layers) down to 2.8x (n=10) |
| n = 12 | excluded — Qulacs threading defect at exactly 12 qubits; at 1 thread the margin is 1.10–1.38x |
| n = 14 | depth-dependent: SF 1.28x at L=1, Qulacs 1.33x at L=64; at 1 thread SF wins all depths (1.5–2.0x) |
| n = 16 | SF 1.05–1.26x, two cases flip under the export-free Qulacs contract |
| n = 18 | SF 1.17–1.73x |
| n = 20 | SF 5.36–7.37x; at L=64, 1.51 s versus 10.77 s |
| dense n=16, 564 → 6,324 gates | SF margin grows 1.08x → 9.65x |

Primary timing uses the same deliverable on both sides: a freshly built
Qulacs `QuantumState`, `update_quantum_state`, and a `get_vector()` export,
versus `sf.simulate()`. An export-free Qulacs variant (`q_core`, no
`get_vector`) is also recorded as a lower bound; it changes the winner in two
n=16 cases. The n=12 row is a measured OpenMP pathology in Qulacs at that exact
size — 0.12 ms at one thread for the shallow circuit, 4.4 ms at four threads for
the same circuit, 292 ms at four threads and 64 layers — not a SuperFermion
advantage, so it is reported separately rather than folded into the win count.

All SuperFermion numbers on this page are 0.1.15. The campaign ran on the
`superfermion_optimized` lane tree, extension SHA-256 `53A476E3…`; the published
`superfermion==0.1.15` wheel is a separate compilation, extension SHA-256
`2D040118…`. Before either was used the two were audited against each other:
identical public surface, and three independent batch paths agree to
`4.7e-16` on identical inputs. (The lane tree's `__version__` string reads
`0.1.14` because the version bump landed after the optimisation work; the code
and extension are the 0.1.15 release. The only API difference between the 0.1.15
and 0.1.14 wheels is `sf.prepare()` and `PreparedCircuit`.)

Why the curve has this shape is explained on
[Why SuperFermion Is Fast](/docs/reference/execution-engine): the planner only
enables fusion and run merging at `2^20` amplitudes (n=20) and above, because
below that size the state is cache-resident and the rewrites measured slower.

## Gradient Benchmarks

### Adjoint Differentiation

The adjoint method computes all parameter gradients in one backward pass,
independently of parameter count (Jones & Gacon 2020).

The grid is an SU(2) ansatz: `RY` then `RZ` on every qubit in each layer, a
ring of `CX` between layers, so `2nL` parameters. The observable is a ring
TFIM, `sum_i Z_i + sum_i X_i X_(i+1)`. SuperFermion is the
`superfermion_optimized` build (extension SHA-256 `53A476E3...`); PennyLane is
0.45.0 on `lightning.qubit` with `diff_method="adjoint"`, driven through
`qml.grad`. Both columns are end-user calls — each framework's own dispatch,
tape and plan construction is inside the timer.

**More qubits, one layer** — milliseconds, median of repeated calls:

| Qubits | Params | Superfermion | PennyLane | Ratio |
|---------|--------|-------------|-----------|-------|
| 4 | 8 | 0.08 | 3.5 | ~43x |
| 6 | 12 | 0.11 | 3.5 | ~32x |
| 8 | 16 | 0.21 | 5.5 | ~27x |
| 10 | 20 | 0.41 | 10.8 | ~26x |
| 12 | 24 | 0.75 | 6.2 | ~8x |
| 14 | 28 | 4.3 | 12.9 | ~3.0x |
| 16 | 32 | 19 | 60 | ~3.2x |
| 18 | 36 | 102 | 367 | ~3.6x |
| 20 | 40 | 924 | 1,742 | ~1.9x |

**More layers** — SuperFermion adjoint, milliseconds:

| Qubits | L=1 | L=2 | L=4 | L=8 |
|---------|------|------|------|------|
| 4 | 0.08 | 0.25 | 0.28 | 0.48 |
| 6 | 0.11 | 0.21 | 0.40 | 1.11 |
| 8 | 0.21 | 0.41 | 0.76 | 1.85 |
| 10 | 0.41 | 0.85 | 1.49 | 3.10 |
| 12 | 0.75 | 2.06 | 4.54 | 8.87 |
| 14 | 4.29 | 8.83 | 18.9 | 38.0 |
| 16 | 19.0 | 46.6 | 84.8 | 151 |
| 18 | 102 | 221 | 598 | 1,294 |
| 20 | 924 | 1,933 | 3,792 | 7,352 |

Eight layers is eight times the parameters of one layer and 6–13x the time:
each layer is also `2n` more gates, and the adjoint pays per gate. What it
never pays is the `2P` factor.

**Against our own parameter-shift, 20 qubits:**

| Layers | Params | Adjoint | Parameter-shift | Ratio |
|---------|--------|---------|-----------------|-------|
| 1 | 40 | 924 ms | 71 s | ~77x |
| 2 | 80 | 1.93 s | 142 s | ~73x |

Both sides grow with depth, so the ratio stays flat. Each parameter costs
parameter-shift two full circuit runs — 160 of them at 80 parameters, against
two sweeps for the adjoint. L=4 and L=8 are not tabulated: one parameter-shift
call at that size takes 5–10 minutes to measure.

**Every case is checked three ways.** Across all 36 grid points the largest
difference between SuperFermion's adjoint and central finite differences is
`9.9e-9` (truncation error of the difference), against parameter-shift
`4.4e-13`, and against PennyLane's adjoint `4.0e-14` absolute, `6.2e-14`
relative. The public API and the raw IR call return bit-identical values.
The gradients themselves were never the weak point of this page.

**How to read the ratio.** At n=4 the 43x is dispatch: one FFI call against
`qml.grad`'s tape construction and autograd transform. It falls to 3.6x at
n=18 and 1.9x at n=20, where both sides pay for a 16 MB state and a 40-term
observable. SuperFermion's adjoint also costs 7–42x its own forward pass at
n >= 14 — 19 ms against 2.8 ms at n=16, 924 ms against 30 ms at n=20 — while
PennyLane's costs about 1x its forward pass. That asymmetry, not the
algorithm, is what flattens the last column. Small-n timings move by up to
~40% between runs (the n=10 and n=12 PennyLane points swap order); the trend
does not.

**Withdrawn.** The earlier version of this table reported 0.1 ms against
80 ms at n=4 — about 800x — and stated that PennyLane's adjoint wins at
n >= 18. Neither survives. The 80 ms does not reproduce: the repo's stored
artifact (`notebooks/benchmark_adjoint_data.json`) records 3.44 ms for the
same point, the notebook's own printed run says 80.24 ms, and this run
measures 3.5 ms. The notebook attributes its number to the PennyLane side
building the observable as nested `qml.prod`, which measures observable
construction, not the adjoint. The n >= 18 loss came from a 0.1.x build that
turned in 579 ms at n=18; the current build turns in 102 ms there.

## Per-Operation Benchmarks

A training step is not one call. This is the same circuit and the same
observable split into the operations a user actually invokes, one layer, 4 to
24 qubits, on the same machine as the tables above.

SuperFermion is timed as `sf.run(circuit, device="cpu", shots=0)` for execution
plus `result.expectation(obs)` for the observable. The second call reads the
Rust state handle directly; the older `obs.expectation(result.statevector)`
exports the state to NumPy first and costs 4–6.5x more — 25.2 s against 4.1 s
for the same 48-term observable at n=24. Every row below uses the native call.

PennyLane's forward cost depends on `diff_method`, by up to 4x at n=24, so both
modes are reported instead of picking the flattering one. `"adjoint"` is what
`qml.qnode(dev)` chooses by default on `lightning.qubit`; `"parameter-shift"`
is cheaper above n=16.

**Same numbers, not just the same circuit.** The timings use a ring TFIM,
which is invariant under relabelling qubits, so it cannot catch a wire-order
mismatch. The grid was therefore re-run with per-qubit angles that differ and
with asymmetric observables: `<Z0>`, `<Z1>`, `<Z0Z1>` and the TFIM agree with
PennyLane's parameter-shift result at every size from 4 to 22 qubits, worst
absolute difference `5.1e-12` on a value of 11.2 (n=22, `4.6e-13` relative).
Both columns simulate the same state at the same wire convention, where the
leftmost character of an observable string is qubit 0. Note that the raw
`result.statevector` array is little-endian (qubit `q` is bit `q` of the
index), unlike `qml.state()`; `result.expectation` handles that for you.

Three things set the shape of these tables.

*The observable is the operation that dominates.* Above n=16 the 2n-term
Hamiltonian is 69–82% of SuperFermion's own call and 72–77% of PennyLane's, so
the `Execute + <TFIM>` ratio measures the expectation-value kernel, not the
simulator. Execution alone is 4.2–5.5x against PennyLane's cheaper mode; the
full call is 3.8–4.9x. Per term at n=24, over 16.8M amplitudes, SuperFermion
spends about 64 ms and PennyLane about 300 ms.

*SuperFermion's fixed cost is not monotonic below 16 qubits.* `sf.run` takes
4.1 ms at n=12 but 3.2 ms at n=16, for 16x less work at n=12; a separate probe
measured 1.1 ms at n=14. Plan construction and dispatch, not the kernel, set
those rows, so read them as ceiling estimates rather than scaling data.

*PennyLane wins one operation: parameter-shift gradients.* Same rule, same
circuit, `2P` evaluations: 0.22 s against 0.10 s at n=12, 2.24 s against
1.02 s at n=16, 12.3 s against 12.1 s at n=18, and 70.7 s against 60.5 s at
n=20. SuperFermion's parameter-shift loop re-exports the state per shift, so
each evaluation costs 0.88 s where `result.expectation` costs 0.21 s — the
adjoint path avoids the loss entirely, which is what the gradient tables above
measure.

**Boundaries of this run.** One host (8 CPU, 7.9 GB RAM, Windows). The n=24 row
is 1–3 repetitions with about ±25% spread; every other row is the median of
3–5 calls. No GPU, hardware backend, or qubit count above 24 was measured in
this section — a 24-qubit state is 268 MB, and 26 would not fit alongside
PennyLane in the RAM available here. Batched parameter sweeps are a separate
run, below.

## Batched Parameter Sweeps

Training does not evaluate one parameter point, it evaluates a bank of them.
`sf.prepare(circuit)` compiles the gate ordering, constants and fusion plan
once and returns a `PreparedCircuit` that runs the whole bank in one call, with
the Python GIL released so a thread pool composes with the inner Rayon pool.
The observable is the ring TFIM used above (`2n` terms); its `⟨X_iX_j⟩` half is
taken as `⟨Z_iZ_j⟩` after `H^⊗n`, which makes it two `diagonal_batch` calls on
the circuit and a rotated copy.

Same circuit, same observable, one process, 4 threads. Milliseconds for the
whole sweep, `Batch` = parameter points evaluated per call:

| Qubits | Batch | SuperFermion | `lightning.qubit` | `default.qubit` | Speedup | Fastest path |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 8 | 2.9 | 25.5 | 89 | 8.9x | `diagonal_batch` x2 |
| 12 | 32 | 10.8 | 67.8 | 331 | 6.3x | threaded `diagonal_batch` |
| 16 | 8 | 42.9 | 187 | 1.65 s | 4.4x | threaded `diagonal_batch` |
| 16 | 32 | 153 | 726 | 5.9 s | 4.8x | threaded `prepare` |
| 18 | 8 | 202 | 2.04 s | 6.5 s | 10.1x | threaded `diagonal_batch` |
| 18 | 32 | 931 | 8.24 s | 24.8 s | 8.9x | `diagonal_batch` x2 |

PennyLane gets the fair baseline: one broadcasted QNode over the bank,
`qml.dot` for the observable, `diff_method=None`, `interface="numpy"`. The two
sides agree on every entry to `2.1e-13` or better.

Which SuperFermion path wins changes with size, and that is worth more than the
constant: `diagonal_batch` twice is fastest at n=12/B=8 and n=18/B=32; threading
the sweep is what wins at n=16 (43 ms against 53 ms unthreaded at B=8, 153
against 214 at B=32); threading *loses* at n=18/B=32,
where the block is already Rayon-parallel and the second pool only adds
contention (1.13 s against 931 ms).

The native batch Pauli call — `Circuit.to_ir()` followed by
`simulate_pauli_expval_batch(...)` — looks like the obvious entry point for a
sweep and is the wrong one. It is the only path here that still rebuilds the
fusion plan on every call, and it needs 12.2 s for the n=18/B=32 sweep.

The naive version of this benchmark — `sf.run()` once per parameter point —
loses to `lightning.qubit` at n=12 (47.9 ms against 25.5, 171 against 67.8) and
wins from n=16 up (102 against 187, 391 against 726, 451 against 2.04 s, 1.92 s
against 8.24 s). Batching on SuperFermion is a property of the prepared paths,
not of looping the public API.

**Where it loses, and what changes it.** Three cases in this sweep go the wrong
way, and all three are path choices rather than engine limits — the same
SuperFermion build wins each one once the right path is used:

| Case | Loses with | Use instead | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| n=12, per-point loop | `sf.run()` per point, 47.9 ms against 25.5 | `sf.prepare()` + `simulate` | 4.3 ms, 5.9x ahead of Lightning |
| n=18/B=32, native batch Pauli | `to_ir()` + `simulate_pauli_expval_batch`, 12.2 s | `prepare()` + `diagonal_batch` x2 | 931 ms, 13x faster |
| n=18/B=32, outer thread pool | `PreparedCircuit` across 4 threads, 1.17 s | one call, no outer pool | 931 ms |

The first row's result is measured against `lightning.qubit`; the other two are
the same build against itself. None of the three is a case where the engine is
out-run — each is a call made outside the compiled path, which is what the
prepared handles exist to remove.

Both columns come from a single process and one host state, and that matters
more than it sounds: absolute milliseconds move with host load between
sessions, and the ratio moves with them. The n=12/B=8 cell has measured 6.2x,
8.9x and 9.2x across three sessions — same script, same host. Read each row as
the middle of a distribution rather than a constant; the ordering of the paths
and the 4.4x–10.1x band are what reproduce.

## Why the Speedup

Five mechanisms, each removing a different cost:

| Mechanism | Removes | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Compile-once handles | DAG walk, matrix rebuild, parameter rebinding on every call | `sf.prepare()`, `sf.cat()` |
| 1q fusion and diagonal / CNOT run merging | whole passes over the `2^n` amplitudes | `sf-ir` instruction plan |
| One in-place buffer | the second buffer's write-allocate traffic and resident footprint | statevector kernels |
| Hand-written AVX2/FMA plus Rayon above measured size floors | scalar arithmetic, and thread overhead below the floor | `sf-ir` kernels |
| Adjoint instead of parameter-shift | the `2P` factor in gradient cost | `sf-ir`, `sf-cat` |

One nuance changes how these numbers should be read: the instruction-plan
rewrites are only enabled from `2^20` amplitudes (n=20) upward. Below that the
state is cache-resident and the same rewrites measured **1.34–2.63x slower** at
n=16–18, so the planner keeps the faithful pair-pass path. That is part of why
Qulacs wins at n ≤ 10 and why the statevector crossover sits around n = 14–16.

The mechanism-level explanation — pair and group transforms, the `CX·D·CX`
rewrite, the Gray-code phase sweep, the GF(2) permutation run, the SIMD shuffle
forms, the `sf.cat` modes, and the memory accounting — is on
**[Why SuperFermion Is Fast](/docs/reference/execution-engine)**.
